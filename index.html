Token boundaries decide what a model predicts

Subword tokenizers are built to compress text. Their boundaries also define the units a language model has to predict, and a compression objective has no reason to respect how a script is written. In Devanagari, Arabic and Tamil, one written unit often spans several codepoints, and BPE is free to cut through the middle of it.

StructPiece is an unsupervised tokenizer that never splits a Unicode Extended Grapheme Cluster (EGC) and steers merges toward affixes it finds in the corpus. It uses no morphological analyzer or other external resource. We study it in two settings: small language models trained from scratch under matched budgets, and vocabulary surgery on a pretrained LLaMA-3.2-1B.

How StructPiece builds a vocabulary

  1. Find affixes in the corpus

    A suffix joins the affix set M when it ends enough distinct word types. The threshold grows with the log of the vocabulary, so frequent endings such as Turkish -lar, -ler, -da survive and accidental ones drop out.

  2. Pretokenize into grapheme clusters

    Text is first split into EGCs: a base codepoint plus its combining marks, matras, virama or diacritics. Merges start from these atoms, so a token can never begin or end inside one.

  3. Score merges with structure in mind

    BPE-style merging continues, with a bonus for merges that produce a discovered affix and a penalty for merges that cross scripts.

S(a, b) = f(a ⊕ b) + λ · 𝟙[a ⊕ b ∈ M] + Ω(a, b)     λ = 2.0,   Ω = −γ · 𝟙[script(a) ≠ script(b)]

Vocabulary surgery on a pretrained model

To swap LLaMA’s vocabulary, each new token is decomposed into the longest matching pieces of the old vocabulary, and its embedding starts as the mean of those pieces. A short round of continued pretraining then aligns the new boundaries with the unchanged Transformer backbone.

A compression–representation frontier

With architecture, vocabulary size (4,000) and token budget (2,048,000) held fixed, the same ordering appears in all nine languages. StructPiece has the lowest token perplexity, SentencePiece Unigram compresses best, and SentencePiece BPE sits between them. StructPiece gives up some compression to get more predictable units.

Vocabulary surgery on LLaMA-3.2-1B

If structurally aligned tokens project more coherently into a pretrained embedding space, they should keep more of the model’s ability after the vocabulary is replaced. At the same 2.6B-token checkpoint, StructPiece keeps 13.0 points more zero-shot accuracy than a 32k BPE vocabulary on morphologically rich languages.

StructPiece improves NER on Hindi, Arabic and Turkish and loses on English, where BPE units already line up with entity spans. POS tagging improves on the three morphologically rich languages and is flat on English.

XNLI stays near chance for every tokenizer. The effect is specific to boundary-sensitive tasks and is not a general reasoning gain.

When to use it, and what it costs

Prefer StructPiece

  • Morphologically rich or non-Latin-script languages
  • Sequence labeling such as NER and POS, where span boundaries matter
  • Vocabulary surgery or domain adaptation from a large pretrained LLM
  • Training from scratch with a small vocabulary (8k or less)

Prefer compression-first BPE

  • Analytic, Latin-script languages, for example English NER
  • Long-context generation, where bits per character matter
  • Inference-constrained settings that cannot absorb longer sequences
  • Large vocabularies (above 32k), where grapheme fragmentation fades
